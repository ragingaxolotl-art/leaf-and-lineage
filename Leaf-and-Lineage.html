    YOUR POCKET FIELD GUIDE

    A small world. Real science ideas.

    Change the habitat color, then press Start evolving. Or use Next generation to take one step at a time. Click any beetle to pause and inspect it.

    Try a little experiment

    1. Predict which colors will blend into the meadow.
    2. Run 10–20 generations. Watch the average color and camouflage.
    3. Switch to dry leaves. Did the beetles change immediately? What changes after several more generations?
    4. Reset. Set selection to 0%. Compare random survival with selection.
    5. Drag two beetles into a jar, inspect their possible offspring, and press Breed jar families. Mutation at 0% makes inheritance easier to follow.

    What is happening?

    Genes are inherited. Each beetle has two copies of three color genes: red, green, and blue. Its visible color uses the average of each pair. An offspring gets one randomly chosen copy per gene from each parent.

    Selection changes who reproduces. In natural mode, 24 of the 48 beetles become breeders. Camouflage increases their chance of being selected, but chance still matters. The bird eats the other 24 beetles, then the surviving breeders produce 48 offspring. Turn off Show the bird eating to shorten the transition; the selection rule stays the same. Reduced-motion settings also shorten the transition.

    Eight jars, eight families

    Drag a beetle into each of a jar’s two slots. You can also select a beetle on a leaf and click an empty slot. The same beetle can be used in different jars, but a pair must contain two different beetles. Drag onto a filled slot to replace a parent, or click it to remove the parent.

    Each jar produces six siblings in its column. Empty jars choose two random parents from their own column; a jar with only one parent must be completed or cleared. Use Use pair in all jars to compare eight families from the same pair. The bird does not eat beetles in jar mode. Jars clear after breeding so that you can choose parents from the new generation. Click a jar’s family label to review the cross that made its current column.

    How do the possibilities work?

    For each color gene, either copy from parent A can combine with either copy from parent B: four equally likely possibilities. The Punnett square shows these four cells. Three independently inherited color genes give 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 equally likely gene combinations before mutation. Some combinations produce the same visible color, so their probabilities add together.

    For example, if both parents have red copies 100 and 200, their babies can have red values 100 (25%), 150 (50%), or 200 (25%). Each baby draws independently. Six siblings may not match these proportions exactly. These RGB genes use additive inheritance; they are a simple teaching model, rather than the actual genetics of beetles.

    Mutations are random. The rate applies independently to each of the six inherited gene copies. A mutation shifts a value up or down by 1–36 (within 0–255). Mutations do not aim for the habitat color.

    Populations evolve. A beetle’s inherited color stays fixed during its lifetime. A habitat change can change its camouflage score immediately, but its genes stay the same. New generations can have different gene frequencies.

    Reading your results

    Average camouflage is a 0–100% color similarity score, not a survival probability. Color diversity measures spread around the population’s average RGB color. The chart records camouflage at each generation’s birth, so habitat switches can cause jumps.

    A useful model, with limits

    Real beetle coloration involves more complex biology. RGB genes, additive inheritance, fixed population size, equal fertility, and camouflage-only selection are teaching simplifications. This model omits dominance, migration, age, sex, and changing population size. Random survival can change gene frequencies even with selection turned off (genetic drift).
