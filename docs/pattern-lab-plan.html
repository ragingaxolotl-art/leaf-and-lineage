Pattern Lab: creative breeding tank, patterns, antennae and legs

Design and implementation notes · October 21, 2026 · Pattern Lab v1 is built as a separate edition.

Keep Simplified v1.0 intact. The current game is preserved at /simplified/, with separate teacher links, an offline copy and a version manifest. Develop Pattern Lab as a separate, breeding-only edition and route. Existing class links must continue to open the correct edition.
Pattern Lab v1: Open the creative breeding tank · Teacher setup. The first implementation has 50 paired loci, a 48-beetle breeder tank, a safe overflow nursery, and a separate ten-favorite Keep tank. Save files retain your favorites, genes, names and family records on your own computer. New broods never replace the Keep tank. The sections below retain the design rationale; the implementation in genetics.js is the executable schema.

1. What “random” means

Offspring get random parental transmissions and occasional mutations. They can have no visible pattern, one pattern color, or two pattern colors. A plain beetle still carries genes for its hidden pattern colors, size and arrangement. Its visible pattern is generated once and remains fixed for its lifetime; opening the inspector, resizing the page or watching it wander must not repaint it.

This is an invented teaching model of inheritance, linkage, gene interactions and selective breeding. We would not present these genes as actual beetle genetics.

2. Traits and genetic rules

TraitProposed data and ruleResult students can observe
Body colorTwo linked color recipes; each includes full-range RGB and expression strength.The stronger recipe supplies the base; equal strengths blend. No fixed color ranking.
Pattern visibilityA suppressor gene S/s: SS or Ss is plain; ss allows the pattern.Plain carriers can pass s and all underlying pattern genes to descendants.
One or two pattern colorsA separate T/t gate: with pattern visible, T_ shows one color and tt permits two.Its gate is on a different chromosome pair from S so the introductory cross is predictable.
Pattern colors 1 and 2Each has its own pair of linked RGB/expression recipes.Colors are inherited even when their layer is hidden. Any RGB color is reachable; there is no tiny fixed palette.
Spots versus mottlesA shape locus controls round spots, irregular spots, or merged soft mottles.A heterozygote can have an intermediate shape. It does not receive a whole unrelated new pattern at birth.
Mark count and sizeA few additive loci control density and radius, with bounds.Several genes contribute, so siblings vary around family tendencies; number and size remain separate.
DistributionAdditive biases for front/back, center/edges, and clustering/spread.Parents pass regional tendencies, rather than an arbitrary list of random pixels.
Symmetry and edge softnessOne or more loci control left/right mirroring, jitter amplitude, and spot edge softness.Variation can produce neatly paired spots or irregular mottling without changing the genes during a redraw.

A practical first version would have roughly 55–75 loci including antennae, legs and movement traits, each with two copies. The exact count should follow a renderer prototype. Use five chromosome pairs with color recipes stored as linked blocks; preserve actual homolog order. Start with an explicitly documented dominance/recessiveness rule for each discrete gene and additive rules for continuous traits. Avoid one global dominance order for colors.

3. A useful classroom cross

Two plain parents can both be Ss Tt, carrying hidden pattern colors. With S and T on separate chromosome pairs and mutation off, the expected offspring are:

Visible outcomeChanceWhy
Plain75%At least one S suppresses the pattern.
One pattern color18.75%ss permits pattern; T_ keeps the second color hidden.
Two pattern colors6.25%ss tt reveals both layers.

Each baby is an independent draw; a small brood need not match these percentages. All pattern colors and arrangements come from the parents’ real gene copies. Mutation is not needed for this surprise.

4. Separate genome, appearance and development

Genome: inherited copies, chromosome/homolog order, locus positions and mutation records. There is no separate “hidden blue” label or ancestral archive: hidden copies are normal genes whose effects are masked.

Phenotype: computed body color and pattern layers, including mark centers, sizes, colors and softness. This can be cached for speed but must remain derived data. A plain coat produces zero visible layers without deleting their genes.

Development seed: stable for that individual, providing small non-inherited variations in mark edges and jitter. Shared genetic layout rules should account for most family resemblance. Developmental noise must never overwhelm inherited placement, or be confused with mutation.

5. How marks get placed

Use fixed candidate anchors in normalized shell coordinates, shared across all beetles. Count/density genes determine how many anchors become active; regional genes bias their ranking toward front, back, edges or center. Keep the anchor rankings correlated when genes change, rather than reseeding every mark after any mutation. Size genes scale the active marks. Symmetry genes mirror positions across the shell seam. A small stable developmental offset makes siblings differ without erasing family resemblance.

Spots use circles or ellipses. Mottles can use a few overlapping, soft-edged lobes per active anchor, so they remain inexpensive to render. The second pigment layer uses a separate mask with explicit overlap order. Clip both layers to the shell. Shared shape data should drive the visible drawing, inspection and exported trait measurements.

6. Inheritance and mutation

For each chromosome pair, produce a gamete by selecting a homolog, with occasional crossover between defined blocks. Keep RGB and expression genes within a pigment recipe strongly linked in the initial version. Each baby gets one gamete from each parent. Locus order and recombination rules must be saved with the genome version.

Keep color, size, count and placement mutations independent of the environment. Numeric mutations make bounded small changes; discrete mutations change an allele. Tune a low per-copy rate after counting the loci: reusing 3% across a much larger genome produces more total changes per baby. A starting experimental range of 0.5–1% per copy is reasonable to test, not a promised final setting. Mutation-off breeding must remain useful.

7. Creative goals rather than survival or reproductive rankings

Children choose the beetle they want, then choose a parent group of two to four beetles in each active jar. There are no birds, automatic mate searches, trait-based fertility bonuses or globally superior shapes in this edition. Backgrounds are viewing backdrops. All valid selected pairs can produce the usual brood, with inherited variation and optional mutation.

Different traits have different design strengths: nubs suit a compact appearance, long threads create a dramatic silhouette, and dense hairs create a fluffy look. These are useful to different personal goals, not automatic numeric advantages. Optional teacher challenges may ask for a visible combination, but free creation is the default. Do not mark a student’s freely chosen beetle as less successful because it differs from a template.

Keep the rendered pattern spatially accurate: averaging red spots and green skin into one brown swatch would hide what the child actually bred. Full stats can show the real inherited copies, actual visible colors, pattern coverage, antenna measurements and family history. None of these statistics changes the chance of a chosen pair breeding.

8. Larger beetles and student data

Give beetles a larger selectable view and a magnified inspector, with clear pattern colors and marks. Use a top-down breeder tank with stable logical bounds. Jars are breeding groups rather than owners of tank rows. Zooming and screen size cannot change parent membership or inheritance. Backdrops are aesthetic context. Provide a clear enlarged view and cooled pause for selecting moving beetles on narrow Chromebooks.

Keep the teacher’s choice to hide or show statistics. Full details should distinguish what is visible, both inherited copies, masked traits, parents, mutations and offspring trait forecasts. Basic students should discover patterns from breeding and observations. Pattern Lab classroom links open jar breeding. The preserved Simplified edition keeps its existing activity choices.

Use exact probabilities for discrete gates such as S/s and T/t. For many continuous linked traits, show explicitly labeled sampled predictions or ranges with a stated sample size, rather than pretending to enumerate every possible whole-genome offspring. Use a separate versioned class-code format such as PL1, containing starting conditions rather than a student’s personal data or full saved history.

Founder presets should deliberately cover a range of hues, hidden copies and pattern arrangements, rather than relying on a small random sample to contain every useful trait. Teacher links store the chosen founder recipe and seed. Keep the new PL1 links separate from old LL1–LL4 links; do not silently reinterpret a Simplified class as a different genome.

9. Build and verify in stages

  1. Genome and specimen viewer: render plain, one-color spots, two-color spots and mottles; prove stable redraws and full color reach.
  2. Breeding laboratory: implement copied alleles, linkage, masked traits, mutation-off crosses and truthful previews. Verify the Ss Tt example and multigeneration hidden traits.
  3. Appendage laboratory: implement independently variable antennae, leg shapes, inherited motor speed and strength; confirm stable appearance and masked-trait inheritance.
  4. Creative breeding tank: connect four-slot jars, original-group broods, the nursery/collection, wandering, cooling pause, enlarged inspection, family records and classroom permissions. Backgrounds remain aesthetic. Keep Simplified unchanged.
  5. Inheritance and device audit: follow multigeneration crosses with mutation off and on, varied starting seeds, small boards and Chromebook performance checks. Validate predicted gene transmissions, masked traits, color, mark size/distribution and antenna combinations. Check that view settings do not affect breeding.

10. Acceptance checks before release

  • Hidden pattern genes survive masking and reappear through real inheritance.
  • The same genome and development seed render identically on redraw and export.
  • Small genetic changes usually preserve recognizable family pattern structure.
  • Rendering, inspection and exports use the same clipped, composited appearance.
  • Color and pattern summaries never conceal the actual separate colors a student bred.
  • No antenna form, color or pattern receives a breeding-success bonus.
  • Mutation-off crosses match their stated probabilities; sampled forecasts are labeled estimates.
  • Speed, zoom and viewport size do not change genetic transmission odds.
  • Simplified class links, saved parent jars and downloads remain intact.
  • Four-slot Pattern Lab jars preserve their original group, and every offspring records its own two actual parents.

11. Antennae for a creative jar-breeding lab

Clarified direction: children choose both parents in the jars and breed toward the beetle they want. This edition has no birds, automatic mate search, reproductive-success bonuses, mandatory score targets or universal best antenna. Different forms have different visual strengths for a chosen design. A teacher may optionally suggest a target, but free creation is the default.

Real-world inspirations

The examples below illustrate real antenna forms, not real gene maps or the ability to cross unrelated species. Non-beetle examples are labeled. Our fictional beetles can combine features inspired by these forms. “Tiny nubs” is an artistic short-length extreme, not a claim that all adult beetles naturally have unsegmented antennae.

Length, shape, hairiness and branching should be separate. Otherwise every feathery antenna would be forced to be long, and a child could not breed a tiny fluffy antenna or an extra-long smooth one.

How children work toward their own beetle

  1. Look at the parents and any family records the teacher allows.
  2. Choose two parents with useful visible or suspected hidden copies.
  3. Breed a brood in that jar and compare siblings.
  4. Keep offspring with desired combinations and select them for a later cross.
  5. Repeat, or change direction freely. Existing jars keep their original parents until deliberately replaced.

Examples of self-chosen goals: a red beetle with purple spots and tiny fluffy antennae; a blue mottled beetle with long smooth antennae; a yellow beetle with symmetrical broad fans. These are examples, not required objectives or fitness rankings.

Genetic data

Add a fourth chromosome pair for antenna-development blocks, bringing the earlier antenna prototype to roughly 40–55 loci before adding the leg and movement module, subject to simplification after rendering tests. Each locus has two real inherited copies. Use expression/modifier genes for the framework and additive genes for measurements. Keep latent branch and hair genes even when a smooth framework or suppressor masks them. No categorical antenna form is globally better or always genetically dominant. Define and test expression rules, including equal-strength cases, before release.

Start with shared genes for both antennae, plus tightly bounded developmental variation that is fixed at birth. The body color, spots, mottles and antennae all remain stable across redraws. Gene transmission and mutation use the same rules regardless of the child’s preferred target. Antenna traits do not alter Mendelian odds, add mutation bonuses, block chosen pairs, or change brood capacity.

Checks for the antenna module

  • Each child’s antenna data can be traced to parental copies or an explicitly logged mutation.
  • Short smooth parents can retain masked branch/hair traits, with surprise offspring only when the actual inherited copies permit it.
  • Length, hair density and branch length can vary independently, within valid rendering bounds.
  • Left/right antennae share the intended gene program; zoom and animation never alter their shape data.
  • Chosen jar groups remain valid regardless of antenna shape; each newborn still has two actual parents, and the selected group stays until deliberately changed.
  • The interface does not call a creative design a failure or rank it against a universal ideal.

12. Top-down breeder tank, cooling pause, four-slot jars and leg genetics

Creative breeding remains the activity. Children choose the parents and their desired beetle. The tank is aesthetic: no predators, fighting, hunger, accidental deaths, automatic mate choice or movement-based reproductive advantage. This is an extension to the planned Pattern Lab, not a change to the preserved Simplified version.

Tank and offspring flow

Show the breeder tank from above, with a clear rim and optional substrate, stones, bark or leaves. Decorations do not change inherited traits or breeding probabilities. After a jar produces a brood, its offspring emerge from a visible nursery area and wander in the tank. Other beetles remain available; the interface must never secretly replace or delete an existing individual.

Start with a manageable active-tank limit, such as 48, while keeping the jar and nursery capacities separately visible. Extra babies wait in a nursery rather than disappearing. Children can move beetles to a separate top-down Keep tank (up to ten favorites) and return them later. An eleventh favorite is refused without deleting anything; later broods leave these ten residents unchanged. A Save Keep tank + family file button downloads the entire experiment for local restoration. Keep-tank and nursery entries retain their IDs, genes, parents, names and saved appearance. Every beetle has one current container, preventing visual duplicates.

Movement and the cooling pause

Wandering uses smooth turns, boundary avoidance and gentle separation. It is a visual animation, not a route to food or mates. Inherited speed affects visible walking pace, while pose and stride can reflect the leg phenotype. Actual tank coordinates, headings and motion phase are runtime state, not inherited genes.

Cool & pause: briefly fade a light blue-white mist over the tank, then leave the beetles still and clearly visible. Freeze positions, headings, legs and antenna animation together. The mist is a pretend cooling effect and cannot alter genes, health or fertility. Honor reduced-motion settings with a static cooling cue instead of moving fog. Keep the effect brief, low contrast and non-obscuring.

Run: resume wandering without creating a new brood. Keep Breed selected jars separate, so starting motion does not automatically breed. Any future automatic-brood control must have its own explicit label. A global animation-speed control changes playback speed only; it does not change the inherited Speed trait or genetic probabilities.

Click, inspect and move to a jar

Clicking a beetle opens a large, stable specimen view showing its actual colors, pattern, antennae and legs. The tank can enter the same cooled-pause state while inspecting, making selection easier. Provide a clear Add to jar action and four visible slots. Pointer dragging is optional; click-to-add and keyboard controls must work too.

A beetle moved into a jar keeps its identity and appears in only that container. Removing it returns it to the tank if space is available, or offers the nursery/collection. Show which beetles are already selected. A jar’s original group remains until the child deliberately replaces or removes a member; offspring do not become parents automatically.

Up to four beetles per jar

Chosen group-breeding rule: each of two independent jars has four parent slots. Children can activate one jar or both, with two to four distinct beetles in each active jar. For each baby, randomly choose two distinct actual parents from that group. Three beetles give three possible unordered pairs; four beetles give six. Each available pair receives equal weight. There is no four-parent DNA averaging: each baby still inherits one copy per gene from each of its own two recorded parents.

10–20 babies per breeding round, total: draw a fresh whole-number brood size from 10 through 20 inclusive. With one active, complete jar, all babies come from that jar. With two active, complete jars, split the total as evenly as possible; randomly give the extra baby to one jar when the total is odd. For example, 16 babies means eight from each jar, not 16 per jar. A jar with fewer than two beetles cannot breed; show it as incomplete before the round and exclude it from the split. If no selected jar is complete, do not start a round.

Separate jars preserve separate crosses. A two-beetle jar produces babies only from that pair. A three- or four-beetle jar mixes pairings independently for each baby, so a brood can contain sibling and half-sibling families. Choose each unordered pair with equal probability, then randomly assign parental transmission order. Never choose a parent from another jar. Shuffle the newborns’ arrival order in the shared tank while retaining each baby’s source jar, actual two parent IDs and brood ID. Sharing a tank does not make beetles mate. Existing parents stay selected until deliberately moved.

Full-stat forecasts must average over the actual possible pairs within each jar, then combine the active jars using their assigned brood shares. They must model the same parent-choice process used for actual births. Ordinary two-parent Punnett probabilities cannot simply be shown for the whole four-beetle group. Brood number counts repeated crossings; an individual’s lineage generation is one plus the greater of its two parents’ lineage generations, so repeated broods of the same pair remain the same generation.

Leg shape and inherited measurements

Keep the normal three pairs of legs. A shared program gives family resemblance; front, middle and hind-pair modifiers allow different shapes on each pair. Store two inherited copies for each modifier. Start with a small set of compatible leg frameworks and continuous parameters, then expand after the renderer is readable at tank scale.

Independent measurements include overall leg length, thigh length and thickness, shin length and width, foot length, joint angle, outward spread, claw size/curve, spine count/length, hair density/length, and front-versus-middle-versus-hind emphasis. Legs can also inherit appendage color and optional banding. Do not assume that long legs must be fast or thick legs must always be strongest.

Speed and strength genes

Speed: use bounded additive motor/stride genes. The displayed movement rate can combine those genes with leg proportions and gait, using documented game units such as body lengths per second. Preserve room for a fast short-legged beetle or a slow long-legged one. Speed affects how the beetle wanders, not which pairs can breed.

Strength: use separate bounded additive muscle/force genes, displayed as a clearly fictional relative index. A thicker or stronger stance may appear in the enlarged specimen view, but strength does not make beetles fight, dominate parents, breed more successfully or erase a weaker child's design. A child can choose to breed for strength as one of their preferred traits.

Keep the genotype source separate from visual movement. A fifth chromosome pair can hold leg-development, motor-speed and strength blocks. The earlier 40–55-locus estimate was before these additions; a combined prototype may need roughly 55–75 loci, to be reduced after determining which measurements require separate genes. A few linked modifiers can often replace many independent loci.

Runtime state and performance

Keep the genome and cached phenotype separate from non-inherited container membership, tank position, heading, leg animation phase and cooling state. Separate wandering randomness from inheritance and mutation randomness, so watching beetles longer cannot consume the genetic random stream. Cache each stable body drawing and use inexpensive transforms for movement. Compute leg detail for the enlarged view without overwhelming a Chromebook.

Save/load should preserve selected jar groups, nursery/collection membership, genes, parentage and individual appearances. Runtime positions can be restored or regenerated without changing a beetle. Background decoration and viewport size must not affect breeding outcomes. No account or server database is needed for local play and downloadable experiment records.

Acceptance checks

  • A jar contains at most four unique living beetles; every newborn has exactly two valid parent IDs.
  • Three- and four-parent group forecasts agree with the implemented pair-choice process.
  • Each round produces 10–20 babies total. One complete jar gets all; two split evenly, with a fair random extra for odd totals.
  • No baby crosses between separate jars, every source jar is recorded, and mixing in the tank never starts a mating.
  • Pausing freezes every beetle and appendage; mist and resume cannot mutate or reroll appearance.
  • A clicked beetle's enlarged view matches its tank drawing and saved genotype.
  • Fast and slow beetles remain equally selectable, using cooling, keyboard controls and click-to-add.
  • Speed and strength inherit and mutate correctly, and do not weight parent choice or fertility.
  • Waiting, frame rate, animation speed, tank background and decoration do not change inheritance probabilities.
  • A full tank preserves extra offspring in the nursery; no silent deletion or duplicate individual occurs.
  • The original jar group stays until deliberately changed, and repeated broods are labeled honestly.
